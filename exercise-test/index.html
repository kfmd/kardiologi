Exercise Stress Test Termination

A simplified HTML summary of when to stop an exercise stress test, divided into absolute stop criteria and relative reasons to consider stopping.

Absolute stop

  • CNS symptoms: ataxia, dizziness, near-syncope.
  • Fall in systolic BP >10 mmHg with rising workload plus other evidence of ischemia.
  • Moderate to severe angina.
  • Signs of poor perfusion: cyanosis or pallor.
  • ST-segment elevation >1.0 mm in leads without preexisting Q waves, except aVR, aVL, and V1.
  • Sustained VT or other serious arrhythmia, including 2nd- or 3rd-degree AV block that impairs cardiac output.
  • Technical difficulty monitoring ECG or systolic blood pressure.
  • Patient asks to stop.

Relative / consider stopping

  • Arrhythmias other than sustained VT, including multifocal ectopy, ventricular triplets, SVT, or bradyarrhythmias that may worsen or affect hemodynamic stability.
  • Bundle branch block that cannot immediately be distinguished from ventricular tachycardia.
  • Claudication, fatigue, leg cramps, shortness of breath, or wheezing.
  • Fall in systolic BP >10 mmHg below baseline with rising workload but without other evidence of ischemia.
  • Exaggerated hypertensive response: systolic BP >250 mmHg or diastolic BP >115 mmHg.
  • Heart rate >85% of age-predicted maximum.
  • Increasing chest pain.
  • Marked ST displacement >2 mm (horizontal or downsloping, 60 to 80 ms after the J-point) in a patient with suspected ischemia.
Tip: think absolute = dangerous enough to stop now; relative = concerning enough to reassess and often stop depending on context.
